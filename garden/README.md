# Garden survey model

A 3D model of the house and garden as they stand (late November 2026), rebuilt from sixty
short phone clips and tied to a measured building survey. It is the "before" picture:
the base for trying out new layouts, and something to hand to an architect or a
landscaper.

- `garden.json` is the site. Every wall, building, tree and object is one entry with a
  position in metres, a size, and a note on how it was measured and how far to trust it.
  `levels.json` is the shape of the ground under it.
- `build.py` turns that file into a Blender scene (`out/garden.blend`), a one-file
  `out/garden.glb`, and the web model the viewer loads (`viewer/model/`).
- `viewer/` is the three.js page: orbit, a north-up plan, layer toggles, a tape measure,
  a mode that puts the 3D camera exactly where the phone was for each still so the
  model can be checked against the footage, and a sun study: the light as it falls on
  the 21st of any month at any hour, turned to the site's own north, to see where sun
  and shade lie.
- `tools/survey/` is the surveying: reconstruction, levelling, control, triangulation.
  `tools/capture/` prints the survey markers, and `CAPTURE.md` says how to photograph a
  site so that the result is accurate.
- `gardenkit/` holds the builders: ground, walls and buildings, plants, objects, and the
  inside of the house.

## Rebuild

```
pip install -r requirements.txt     # Python 3.13: Blender as a module, COLMAP, OpenCV
python build.py                     # about a minute and a half
python tools/serve.py               # preview at http://localhost:8765
```

`build.py` also runs inside Blender: `blender --background --python build.py`.
Textures are generated by code into `textures/`; nothing is downloaded. Everything the
build writes (`textures/`, `out/`, `viewer/model/`) is ignored by git, so a fresh clone
has to run `build.py` once before the viewer has anything to show.

## The site grid

All coordinates are metres in one grid:

- **Origin**: the outer south-east corner of the rear outshot at ground level, by the back
  door. It is a hard corner that is easy to find again with a tape.
- **+X** runs south, away from the back of the house. **+Y** runs east, towards the road.
  **+Z** is up; z = 0 is level 20.00 m on the measured survey's datum.
- Grid north is the direction (-0.943, -0.334) in (X, Y): the back of the house faces
  about 20 degrees west of south. The viewer's plan view is drawn north-up.
- `rot` is degrees anticlockwise seen from above, measured from +X.

**Ground levels.** The ground is not drawn flat. `levels.json` holds its height above
the datum on a quarter-metre grid (made by `tools/survey/terrain.py`), and `build.py`
gives the ground mesh that shape and stands everything on it. A feature is placed in plan
only; how it meets the ground follows from what it is, and `"seat"` in its entry
overrides that:

- `drape`: every point of it is raised by the ground beneath that point. Walls, hedges,
  edgings, beds and anything else drawn from a path or an outline, and soft things that
  lie on the ground (a tarpaulin, a heap of tiles).
- `rigid`: lifted as a whole by the ground under its position, staying upright and
  level. Furniture, pots, trees, sheds.
- `none`: it states its own level. The house and everything inside it (the datum is
  their floor) and anything given a third coordinate in `at` (a spot level, a ball
  sitting on a stump).

Edit `garden.json` and run `build.py` again to change the model. Remove an entry to
remove the thing; copy one to add another. The available `type`s are the keys of
`BUILDERS` in `build.py`, and their parameters are the arguments of the functions in
`gardenkit/`. In Blender every feature is a named object in a collection per layer, so
things can also be moved or hidden by hand.

Layers: Ground, Boundaries, Buildings, Planting, Furniture and objects, Surroundings,
the storeys of the house interior (when `interior.json` is present), plus two that
are information rather than things. **Survey data** holds the plot boundary
from the block plan, the spot levels and inspection chambers from the measured survey and
the grid origin. **Still to survey** hatches the parts nobody has filmed and plants a
numbered marker wherever more footage would help, with what to film in its note.

## Inside the house

`gardenkit/interior.py` draws the inside of a building the way a floor plan does: walls as
solid blocks at their true thickness with openings for doors and windows, floors at their
own levels, stairs, ceiling beams, roof timbers and fixed fittings. In the viewer,
**First floor** and **Ground floor** slice the whole model at ceiling height (4.11 m and
2.16 m above datum) so the rooms can be looked down into, or read as a true floor plan in
Plan view. **Attic** slices nothing: it takes the house's own roof off above the attic
floor. Each room has a card with its size, floor level and headroom.

The description of the rooms themselves lives in `interior.json`, beside `garden.json` and
in the same format, and is **not in this repository**: the layout of the inside of a home
is nobody else's business. `build.py` adds it to the model when the file is there and
carries on without it when it is not. It was drawn from the measured floor plans (wall
faces read off the drawing's own vector lines, so good to a centimetre or two), with
finishes and fittings from a set of interior clips.

## How it was measured

1. **Reconstruction.** COLMAP structure-from-motion on a pan from the lawn and 59 short
   walks: 1,888 frames, 900,000 points, every camera position and direction.
   `sfm.py` does one clip, `sfm_add.py` registers further clips into it (landscape or
   upright, same day or not), `sfm_refine.py` lets everything settle together.
2. **Vertical** from brick coursing (`gravity.py`). Brick walls are laid level, so the
   mortar joints of two walls that are not parallel fix true vertical. Three walls agree
   to 0.2 degrees. The lawn is not level, so fitting a plane to it, as the first version
   of this model did, was 1.5 degrees out.
3. **Scale, position and height** from control (`calibrate.py`, `control/control.json`).
   Eight corners and jambs that can be found both in the footage and on the measured
   building survey (1:50 plans, 1:100 elevations) fix the similarity; they fit to
   5.8 cm rms. Five surveyed spot levels then check the heights: all within 4 cm.
4. **The far side of the garden** was only ever filmed from one spot, which gives good
   directions and poor distances. Those distances were corrected by sighting the same
   things from a second viewpoint (`triangulate.py`, `control/sightings.json`).
5. **The house itself** is drawn from the measured survey, not from the footage.
   The plot boundary and north come from the 1:500 block plan.
6. **Through a window** (`window_view.py`, `control/attic_window.json`). A clip filmed
   from inside cannot be joined to the outdoor reconstruction, but the window is a known
   place and its joinery gives the camera's direction: uprights point straight down,
   level edges along the wall. One frame through the attic window fixed the east wall of
   the outbuilding and how far back it runs, which nothing filmed in the garden can see.
   The check that the method works is built in: the two directions must come out square
   to each other, and here they are 89.7 degrees apart.

7. **Walks that leave the survey and come back** (`sfm_part.py`, `place.py`). Hanging new
   frames one at a time on a survey that is held fixed works while they overlap it. A
   walk round the back of the outbuilding does not: each frame is placed from points made
   by the frames just before, its scale is free to creep, and nothing can spread the
   error. The first attempt put the 5.2 m back wall of the outbuilding at 40% of its
   length. Such a walk is reconstructed by itself, free, together with the sound clips at
   either end of it, and the result is laid over the survey through the cameras the two
   share (156 of them here, agreeing to 1.8 cm). A piece that shares no cameras can be
   placed from sightings instead: frames already in the survey that recognise its points
   give lines of sight they must lie on, and its size comes from something in it of known
   length (`brickscale.py` reads it off the coursing of a brick wall).
8. **Walls** (`elevation.py`). The points on a rendered wall lie within a centimetre or two
   of a straight line, which fixes the wall in plan. Every frame that faces it is then
   projected onto that plane to make a straightened photograph to scale, and the doors
   and windows are read off it.
9. **Ground levels** (`terrain.py`). The lowest survey points in each quarter-metre cell,
   with buildings cut out and shrubs planed off, tied to the 22 spot levels of the
   measured survey. Before tying, the surface agreed with them to 7 cm.
10. **Checking the survey against the model** (`fit.py`). Every survey point is compared
   with the built model: how far is it from the nearest modelled surface? Two lists come
   out. Points with nothing near them, gathered into clusters, each with a crop of the
   footage that shows it: things missing from the model or drawn in the wrong place (a
   hedge that carries on past the gate, a bulk bag a metre from where it stands), and
   things that two clips put in two places. And for each feature, how much of its surface
   has points on it: one that several frames looked at and that has none is not where the
   model says. As it stands 68% of 838,000 points lie within 0.1 m of a modelled surface
   and 84% within 0.2 m. Most of the rest is a parked car, the contents of the
   outbuilding and branches overhanging from outside the plot.
11. **Anchoring to a building** (`anchor.py`). The first run of that check had the front
   wall of the house 0.3 m from where the clips of the front garden showed it. Clips are
   joined to the survey one frame at a time, and along a walk with a weak stretch in it
   the placing slips and carries everything after it along. Nothing inside the survey can
   show that, because the clips still agree with one another. A building drawn from a
   measured survey can. The points that belong to the clips in doubt (each point
   remembers the clip that saw it most) are laid on the building's faces by the turn,
   slide and, if need be, change of size that fits best, each point pulled square on to
   the nearest face and never along it. Seven clips of the front garden had come out 8%
   small, turned 2.2 degrees and 0.4 m out; afterwards their points lie 4 cm from the
   walls. The clips round the back of the outbuilding had slipped 0.32 m along it, which
   takes its east side from 7.15 m to 6.82 m: the figure the sighting through the attic
   window (6) had given all along. A flat wall holds a walk square to itself and leaves
   it free to slide along; frames at one end of the walk that a free reconstruction (7)
   ties to sound clips hold that (`--hold`).
12. **Sightings of known things** (`anchor.py --sights`, `control/east_sights.json`). A
   clip that hangs on the survey by one neighbour can swing. One filmed along the east
   wall showed the well across the front lawn 1.3 m from where the clips that walked
   round it had it. It sees too little wall to be laid on the house, but it does see
   things whose places are known: a corner of the house, the well, the foot of a tree.
   Each is picked in a few of its frames, and the clip is turned and slid until every one
   lies on the line of sight through its pick. It had swung 4.3 degrees; eleven picks of
   three things then agree to 2 cm. Only the direction of a sighting is used, never the
   distance. The second half of the same clip, after the camera pans round, would not fit
   the same correction, so it and the clip that hangs on it are left out of the survey
   (`align.py --without c3426:86-999,c3428`). Frames that can be checked against nothing
   do a survey more harm than good: their points go into the ground levels like any
   others.

The first version of this model sized itself on "standard" objects (brick courses, a
door, paving flags) and came out 27% too big near the house. Guessing a scale is not
good enough; control is.

## How far to trust it

| Where | Roughly |
| --- | --- |
| House, old shed, apple tree, drains, spot levels (measured survey) | a few centimetres |
| Outbuilding (walked all round) | front and the first 4 m of the east side 2 cm, back corners 0.15 m, openings 2 to 3 cm; the partition inside is a judgement |
| Patio, east lawn, kitchen garden, gate path, drive, front garden, passage by the shed (filmed on foot) | 0.1 to 0.2 m |
| East end of the south wall and the shrubs by the bench | 0.1 to 0.2 m |
| West wall, stock wall, log pile, picnic table and the rest of the far south-west corner (first clips, seen from two viewpoints) | 0.3 to 0.5 m |
| Ground levels on open ground that was filmed on foot | a few centimetres |
| Ground levels under shrubs and behind buildings; the lane beyond the hedge | interpolated; the lane was seen only at the gate |
| Plot boundary from the block plan | about 1 m |
| Inside the house (measured floor plans) | a few centimetres |
| West side of the outbuilding, the small shed, the north boundary, inside the old shed | glimpsed or not surveyed |

Every feature carries `confidence`: `measured` (drawings, or two viewpoints),
`estimated` (one viewpoint, or partly hidden) or `assumed` (not seen). The viewer can
colour the model by it. Tree crowns are generic; species are mostly not identified.

Disagreements between sources are left visible rather than papered over. The west wall
runs about 1.2 m outside the block-plan boundary. The outbuilding, now measured on all
four sides, is 5.50 m wide where the block plan draws 5.0, and stands 1 to 2 m further
west (the two agree on how deep it is). The paved strip that ends the drive lies 0.5 to
0.8 m outside the mapped boundary. And a small shed stands 2 to 3 m beyond the mapped
line at the edge of the field. A 1:500 map is good to about a metre, so only the last of
these is more than the map's own tolerance: it wants checking on the ground.

### Making it better

`SURVEY_TODO.md` is the running list of what to film and measure next, in order of
usefulness; its numbers match the markers in the viewer. `CAPTURE.md` is how to do it:
full-resolution photographs in place of video, loops that close, and printed markers
(`capture/markers_a4.pdf`) left about the site as fixed points. `markers.py` finds the
markers in the frames by itself, fixes them on the grid, checks the survey's size
against taped distances between them, and hands them to `place.py` as control, so that
a set of photographs reconstructed on its own is laid on the grid by the markers it
shows. Tried on the survey's own frames with sheets pasted in at known places: read
nine times in ten once a marker's side covers 30 pixels and never misread; fixed to 1 to
6 mm from frames of video; distances between them right to 3 mm (5 at worst) over as much
as 17 m; and a
separately reconstructed set of 238 frames laid on the grid by seven markers alone to
within a millimetre of where a hundred shared cameras put it. In general:

- **Tape measurements.** A distance from the grid origin (or between any two things in
  the footage) goes into `control.json` under `distances` and is used by `calibrate.py`.
  The most useful ones run from the origin out to the far walls.
- **Walk, do not pan.** Distances come from moving the camera sideways. Slow loops with
  the ground in frame, overlapping what has already been filmed, beat any amount of
  turning on the spot.
- **Keep more than one thing in the picture.** Three clips filmed close against a brick
  wall could not be joined to anything: a frame full of bricks could be anywhere along the
  wall. Stand back and film along a wall, not at it.
- **Keep the house in the picture when you can.** It is the one thing whose shape is
  known to the centimetre. A clip that shows a wall or two of it can be checked against
  it and put right (11 above); a clip of nothing but lawn and shrubs has to be taken on
  trust.
- **Sight things twice.** Anything that matters can be fixed by picking it in two frames
  taken a few metres apart and adding it to `sightings.json`. It is worth doing even for
  things that look settled: a second set of clips, filmed close to a tree stump that the
  first set had only seen from across the garden, moved it by 1.7 m.

## What is not in the repository

This repository is public, so nothing captured by the camera is committed: not the video,
not the stills the viewer fades against (`viewer/frames/`, `viewer/frames.json`), not the
survey point cloud. The drawings are not here either, only site-grid dimensions read off
them, and the inside of the house (`interior.json`) is left out altogether. Without the
stills the viewer simply hides "Compare with video"; the model itself needs none of them.
To bring the survey back from the original clips
(`pip install pycolmap opencv-python`, and `ffmpeg` on the path):

```
ffmpeg -i pan.mov   -q:v 2 -start_number 0 frames/f_%04d.jpg
ffmpeg -i walk1.mov -q:v 2 -start_number 0 walk1/g_%04d.jpg
ffmpeg -i walk2.mov -q:v 2 -start_number 0 walk2/h_%04d.jpg
S=tools/survey
python $S/sfm.py frames work 2 10                         # the pan: about 25 min on 4 cores
python $S/align.py work/sparse/0 first.npz 1.96           # a working frame; the scale is fixed later
python $S/sfm_add.py work work2 walk1 walk2               # register the walks into it
python $S/sfm_refine.py work2 work3                       # free bundle adjustment
python $S/align.py work3/sparse refined.npz --like first.npz
python $S/gravity.py refined.npz level.npz --courses $S/control/walls.json frames walk1 walk2
python $S/calibrate.py level.npz survey.npz $S/control/control.json frames walk1 walk2
python $S/triangulate.py survey.npz $S/control/sightings.json frames walk1 walk2
```

Clips filmed later join the same grid without redoing any of that. They can be upright
or landscape, and from another day in different light. Frames are named
`<clip>_<number>.jpg`, one directory per clip:

```
ffmpeg -i east1.mov -q:v 2 -start_number 0 east1/i_%04d.jpg
python $S/sfm_add.py work3 work4 east1 east2 east3                  # existing cameras stay put
python $S/align.py work4/sparse survey2.npz --like survey.npz       # lands straight in the site grid
python $S/window_view.py attic_frame.jpg $S/control/attic_window.json
```

A big batch goes in the same way. `dir:10` takes the sharpest frame of every ten, which
is plenty for a walk; with more than four clips the frames to compare are found by
image retrieval instead of trying everything against everything; and `--then` splits
the job into stages, each held fixed for the next, so the run time grows with the
footage and not with its square. `--standing f` deals with the first clip, the pan: what
only it saw, at any distance, is dropped before the new clips are placed, because a new
frame placed against points that are 10 to 30% too close ends up in the wrong place:

```
python $S/sfm_add.py work4 work5 --standing f c3350:10 c3351:10 ... --then c3354:10 ... --then c3375:10 ...
python $S/align.py work5/sparse survey3.npz --like survey2.npz
python $S/export_frames.py survey3.npz viewer $S/control/clips.json frames walk1 walk2 east1 ... c3350 ...
```

Not every clip latches on at the first attempt. A walk breaks off where the camera turns
a corner faster than one frame in ten can follow, and a clip that looks back across the
garden from the far wall may recognise nothing but the pan, whose far points have just
been thrown away. A second pass mends both: give the clips that are missing again with a
smaller step (the frames already placed stay, the new ones fill the gaps), and name the
pairs of clips that you know show the same place, which are then compared in full:

```
python $S/sfm_add.py work5 work6 --link c3362:c3363 --link c3385:c3386 ... c3362:4 c3363:4 c3386:5 ...
```

`--resume N` carries on a run that was stopped, from stage N, without extracting or
matching again.

A walk that leaves the surveyed part and comes back is better reconstructed by itself
and laid over the survey afterwards (see "How it was measured", 7). Give the sound clips
at either end of it as well, so that the piece and the survey share cameras:

```
python $S/sfm_part.py work6 back c3375 c3376 ... c3385 c3386 c3387 c3390 ... c3401
python $S/place.py back/0 survey3.npz survey4.npz --shared c3375,c3376,...,c3385 --add c3386,c3387,...,c3401
```

A piece that shares nothing with the survey is placed from sightings: frames already in
the survey that recognise its points. Sightings from one spot (the pan) say which way the
piece lies, and how far only once its size is known. `brickscale.py` reads the size off
the coursing of a brick wall when a clip shows one squarely. Failing that, try a few
sizes: at the right one the piece's own ground comes out level with the survey's. Here
the bricks gave 0.79 m per unit for one clip of a piece and the ground 0.76 for the clip
next to it, which is as well as two clips of a walk that later proved to be bent could
be expected to agree:

```
python $S/sfm_part.py work6 south c3362 c3363 c3364 c3365
python $S/brickscale.py south/0 c3363 --frames 0:60 --out wall.png -- c3363
python $S/place.py south/0 survey4.npz survey5.npz --db work6/db.db --clips c3362 --scale 0.76
```

`place.py` reports how well the sightings fit. A piece that fits some sighting frames to
a pixel and others not at all is not rigid: it has a weak hinge where two clips barely
overlap, and each length has to be placed by itself (`--clips`). That is what happened to
the walk along the south wall: its first clip went in (sightings within 1.4 px), the
other three, filmed close against the brickwork, came out bent and were left out.

Then the surface and the checks:

```
python $S/terrain.py survey5.npz terrain.npz --levels $S/control/levels.json --buildings garden.json --json levels.json
python $S/elevation.py survey5.npz front.png -3.00,-18.38 -2.89,-12.91 --z -0.2,2.8 --res 0.005 --clips c3375,c3376 -- c3375 c3376
python $S/overlay.py survey5.npz garden.json c3376_0118 check.jpg -- c3376
```

Then the model is built (`build.py`) and the survey is checked against it, point by
point. `fit.py` prints the clusters of points that have no surface, with the features
nearest each, and the features with least of their surface confirmed; it writes a plan
(`fit_plan.png`), the lists (`fit.json`) and a crop of the footage for each of the largest
clusters:

```
python $S/fit.py survey5.npz viewer/model fit --garden garden.json --sheets 24 -- frames walk1 ... c3350 ...
```

A cluster is one of four things: something not modelled (add it, or leave it and say
so), something modelled in the wrong place (move it), a ghost (a clip that turns on the
spot, or looks from far off, puts points at the wrong distance; reflections in window
glass appear behind the wall), or a clip that is itself in the wrong place. Look at the
footage, and at which clips the points come from, before changing anything: saplings
that clips filmed 12 m away put beside the gate path stand a metre and a half further
back in the clips filmed next to them. The last shows as the same thing
twice, or as a wall of the house standing off its own points. `anchor.py` deals with it
(see "How it was measured", 11 and 12):

```
python $S/anchor.py d.npz viewer/model e.npz --to house --clips c3404,c3405,c3406,c3407,c3408,c3409,c3410 --size --lift --hold ref.npz
python $S/anchor.py e.npz viewer/model f.npz --to house --plane -3.2,-12.93,-10.2,-13.20,0.2,2.5 --clips c3387,c3390,c3391,c3393,c3399,c3400,c3401 --carry c3394,c3395,c3396,c3398 --no-turn
python $S/anchor.py f.npz viewer/model g.npz --to house --no-turn --clips c3425 --carry c3426
python $S/anchor.py g.npz viewer/model survey.npz --sights $S/control/east_sights.json --clips c3426
```

Read what it prints before believing it. It gives the distance of the points from the
faces before and after, the turn, slide and change of size, how much of the pull came
from faces looking each way, and how firmly the weakest direction is held: a group that
sees one flat wall is pinned square to it and free to slide along, and is left where it
was in that direction. So the first line above frees the size and the height (`--size`,
`--lift`) only because nine frames at the end of the walk have known places to hold it
by (`--hold`: a copy of the survey made without the clips in doubt, with a free
reconstruction of the last of them laid over it by `place.py`). The second keeps the
group's direction (`--no-turn`), because its one run of house wall is too short to judge
a turn by, and adds a wall the model does not have (`--plane`: the east wall of the
outbuilding, as fitted to the points of clips that are not in doubt). `--carry` moves
clips along with the group without giving them a say: the ones filmed indoors on the
same walk, whose points lie on the inside of the walls. Height stays locked unless
`--lift` is given: walls say nothing about it, and left free it followed sills and eaves
to an answer 0.18 m out against the measured spot levels.

The picks for `--sights` are made like any others (`gridcrop.py`), in the group's own
frames, of things whose places are known from sound clips or from the measured house.
Three things well apart are enough if one of them is near. More picks, from both ends of
the clip, show whether the clip is sound in itself: here the gate posts, picked in its
last frames, missed by 0.4 m after a correction that fitted its first frames to 2 cm,
and that half of the clip was left out.

The whole chain behind the survey as it stands, from the registered model on:

```
python $S/align.py work6/sparse a.npz --like survey2.npz --without c3411,c3412,c3428,c3426:86-999,c3425:206-999
python $S/place.py back/0 a.npz b.npz --shared c3375,...,c3385 --add c3386,c3387,...,c3401
python $S/place.py south/0 b.npz c.npz --db work7/db.db --clips c3362 --scale 0.76
python $S/place.py front/2 c.npz d.npz --shared i --add c3411,c3412
# the four anchor.py lines above, then:
python $S/terrain.py survey.npz terrain.npz --levels $S/control/levels.json --buildings garden.json --json levels.json
```

`export_frames.py` writes the stills and camera poses behind the viewer's "Compare with
video". `control/clips.json` lists the walks to show, the frames to leave out (someone
in shot) and the things to blur wherever they appear (a number plate, a name board).

The reading tools: `plan.py` is the library the others share (pixel to ray, ray to
ground or wall, plan mosaics) and `measure.py` a small command line on top of it.
`gridcrop.py` cuts a piece out of a frame with a pixel grid over it, for the pixel
positions that `control.json`, `sightings.json` and `attic_window.json` need.
`groundgrid.py` goes the other way: it draws the site grid on the ground in a frame, so
anything lying on the ground (an edging, a path, the foot of a post) can be placed by
eye to a quarter of a metre or better. Give it the ground surface (below) rather than a
height: on the drive, a grid drawn a quarter of a metre too high reads a metre out.

Five more work on the survey as a whole:

- `terrain.py` makes the **ground surface** from the survey's own points: the lowest
  points in each quarter-metre cell, with buildings cut out and shrubs planed off, tied
  to the spot levels of the measured survey (`control/levels.json`; the surface agreed
  with them to 7 cm before it was tied). It writes `levels.json`, which `build.py` reads.
- `elevation.py` makes a **straightened photograph of a wall**, to scale, by projecting
  every frame that faces the wall onto its plane. Whatever lies in the plane comes out
  sharp; window and door openings are then read straight off a ruled picture, to a
  centimetre or two. The plane itself comes from fitting a line to the points on the
  wall, which for a rendered wall lie within 2 cm of it.
- `overlay.py` draws the model as lines over any frame: the check that needs no
  rendering. If the house's wireframe sits on the house, the camera is right; if a wall
  is then drawn off its picture, the wall is wrong in the model.
- `fit.py` is the **check of survey against model**: every point against the nearest
  modelled surface. It reads the web copy of the model that `build.py` writes, so what
  is checked is what the viewer shows. Plants count as explained within 0.6 m, since the
  model only sketches them; hard surfaces within 0.3 m.
- `export_colmap.py` writes the survey as a **COLMAP dataset on the site grid**, pictures
  undistorted and cameras as pinholes: the input a Gaussian-splat trainer wants. What
  is trained from it comes out on the model's own grid.
- `anchor.py` **moves a group of clips** (frames and the points that belong to them)
  onto a building of known shape or onto sightings of things with known places, and
  leaves the rest of the survey alone. `align.py` records which clip each point belongs
  to, and can leave clips, or part of one, out of a survey altogether (`--without`).
