# Capturing the site: how to photograph for an accurate, realistic model

`SURVEY_TODO.md` says *what* still needs capturing. This is *how*, for anyone holding the
camera. It replaces "film a short clip" with something closer to what surveyors and game
studios do, and it needs nothing more exotic than a phone, a printer and a tape.

## Three different kinds of "better"

A model can improve in three ways, and each wants different pictures.

| | What it means | What it needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Position** | things are where the model says, to a centimetre or two | fixed reference points (markers), a few taped distances, photographs that tie the whole site together |
| **Shape** | walls lean as they lean, the ground rises as it rises, a tree has its own trunk | sharp, high-resolution, heavily overlapping photographs of every surface |
| **Look** | brick looks like *this* brick, light falls as it does at four o'clock in June | photographs of each material in flat light, and a note of what every plant is |

The first model was built from short clips of phone video. That was enough to place
things to 10 or 20 cm and no better, for three reasons that this guide removes: the
frames were small (under 3 megapixels), the clips were short and barely connected, so
some drifted by half a metre, and nothing on the ground had been measured.

## What a photograph is worth

Depth comes from seeing the same thing from two places. Turning on the spot gives none;
stepping sideways gives it all. How fine a detail can be placed depends on how much
ground one pixel covers:

| Distance to the subject | Frame of phone video (1920 px) | 24-megapixel still | 48-megapixel still |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 m | 1.3 mm | 0.5 mm | 0.3 mm |
| 5 m | 3.1 mm | 1.2 mm | 0.9 mm |
| 10 m | 6.3 mm | 2.4 mm | 1.7 mm |
| 20 m | 12.6 mm | 4.9 mm | 3.4 mm |

A still also has no motion blur worth the name, no video compression and no
stabiliser bending the picture. **Take photographs, not video**, wherever position or
shape matters. Video remains useful for getting the lie of a place quickly.

## Kit

- The phone, on its main lens (1×). Clean the lens.
- 16 to 24 printed markers (`capture/markers_a4.pdf`, one to a sheet of A4), each stuck
  flat to something stiff: card, a clipboard, a square of plywood. Masking tape, or
  stones to weight the ones on the ground.
- A tape of 20 m or more, or a laser distance measure. A ruler.
- A pole, if the roof and the tops of things are wanted: a decorator's extension pole
  with a phone clamp and a remote shutter puts the camera 4 to 6 m up.
- Pencil and paper for the distances.

## Markers

A marker is a pattern software can find by itself in any photograph, tell from every
other marker, and pin down to a fraction of a pixel. Left in place, markers are the
pegs the whole survey hangs on: every photograph showing one is tied to the same fixed
points, so walks cannot drift, and photographs taken weeks apart land on each other.

**Print** at 100% ("actual size", not "fit to page"). The line on each sheet must
measure 100 mm; check one sheet with a ruler.

**Place** them once, for the whole campaign:

- on things that do not move: walls, posts, paving, a slab on the lawn. Not on gates,
  doors, bins, plants or anything that will be tidied away;
- all round the site and through the middle of it, so that wherever you stand two or
  three are in view; three or four on each face of each building;
- at different heights: some on the ground, most at chest height, a few as high as you
  can reach. A spread of heights is what holds the survey level;
- flat, and not under anything shiny. A sheet that curls reads as a different shape;
- note in a few words where each number is ("07: left of the outbuilding door"). If
  one falls or is moved, put up a sheet with a *new* number rather than the old one.

**Measure** about a dozen distances between markers, each from the corner with the
arrow to the corner with the arrow. Long ones are worth far more than short ones:
across the full length and width of the site, corner to corner, and round the
buildings. Write them as `7 to 12: 14.385 m`. Two markers one above the other on the
same wall, with the height between them taped, check the vertical.

## Camera settings

- Main lens, 1×. No zoom of any kind (a "2×" on a single-lens phone is a crop).
- Full-resolution stills: 24 megapixels for everything, 48 where fine detail is the
  point (joinery, brickwork, a roof from the ground). HEIF is fine.
- Live Photos, flash, night mode, portrait mode and filters off.
- Stand still for each exposure. Hold the phone level, landscape for most things,
  upright for tall ones; mixing the two is fine.
- For a run along one wall, press and hold on the screen to lock focus and exposure so
  the wall does not change brightness from frame to frame.
- Keep the originals and send those. Messaging apps and "share as link" shrink
  pictures. Do not crop, straighten or edit.

## How to shoot

Four rules cover almost everything:

1. **Move sideways, never just turn.** Every surface should be seen from at least
   three places, spread over a fair angle.
2. **Overlap by four fifths.** Step no more than a quarter of the distance to the
   subject between photographs: at 4 m from a wall, one pace.
3. **Close the loop.** Finish each walk where it began, and make walks cross each
   other. An open-ended walk is a drawbridge with one end in the air.
4. **Keep markers in the picture.** A photograph with two markers in it is worth
   several without.

**A ten-minute trial comes before all of it.** Print two markers, tape them a few
metres apart on one wall, measure between their arrowed corners, and take twenty
photographs of that wall as described under "Walls and buildings" below. Send those.
They prove the whole chain on real paper and real photographs (print size, glare, file
format, how the phone was held) before an hour is spent on the full walk.

The shoots, in the order worth doing them:

- **The network** (once, first): with all markers up, walk the whole site, round the
  boundary and across the middle, a photograph every step or two. Then each marker by
  itself: square-on from 2 m, and from 30° either side. Three to five hundred
  photographs. This fixes every marker and joins every part of the site to every other.
- **Walls and buildings**: a row of photographs square-on from 3 to 5 m, a pace apart;
  the same row again looking 30° to the left and to the right; then walk round each
  corner in eight or ten small steps so that neighbouring walls are joined. A second
  row from the pole, looking slightly down, gets sills, lintels and the eaves.
- **Ground, paths and beds**: walk in lanes two metres apart, as if mowing, phone at
  chest height tilted 30 to 45° down. From the pole this is better still.
- **Single things** (a well, a shed, a tree trunk): two rings round it at different
  heights, sixteen or more photographs a ring.
- **Roofs and chimneys**: from the pole, all the way round; and from as far back as the
  site allows.
- **Indoors**: lights on, curtains open. From each corner of the room and the middle of
  each wall, at two heights; walk through every doorway photographing each half step so
  that rooms join. Mirrors and windows confuse the reconstruction: include as little of
  them as you can.
- **Materials**, for the look: each kind of brick, render, tile, timber, paving and
  gravel square-on from a metre or so, in shade, with a ruler or a marker in the
  picture. And for each tree and shrub worth naming: the whole plant, the bark, a leaf.

## Light, weather and things that move

- Bright overcast is ideal: no hard shadows, nothing blown out. The middle of the day,
  not the ends of it. Low sun paints long shadows into every texture and flares into
  the lens.
- Shoot one area in one go. Shadows that move between two rows of photographs are
  taken for different things.
- Dry and still: wet surfaces mirror the sky, and wind moves every leaf between frames.
- Move the car, or leave it exactly where it is for the whole shoot. The same for
  bins, bags, tools, garden chairs. Keep people and animals out of the picture.
- Bare branches show a tree's structure; full leaf shows what it looks like. Both are
  worth having, months apart.

## What to send

One folder per shoot, named by date and area, the original files, and a text note with
the marker notes and the taped distances. Photographs carry the place they were taken
in their metadata, and show the house: keep them private. Nothing captured by the
camera goes into this repository.

A thorough capture of a house and garden is two to four thousand photographs, 5 to
10 GB. That is too much to pass through a chat a file at a time, so it wants one of:

- **work where the photographs are**: run the session on the computer that holds them.
  Nothing is uploaded, the photographs never leave the machine, and its graphics
  processor is there for the dense and photoreal reconstructions, which a rented cloud
  machine without one cannot do. An Apple Silicon Mac on macOS 14 or later, or a PC
  with a recent graphics card, is enough. `CLAUDE.md` and `requirements.txt` are what a
  session needs to set itself up there; photographs go in `garden/private/photos/`,
  one folder per shoot, which git ignores;
- **a private store the session can read**: a private repository or drive holding the
  originals and the survey's working files;
- **direct upload** for small, targeted sets: single photographs are well under any
  per-file limit.

## What happens to them

```
python tools/capture/make_markers.py capture/markers_a4.pdf          # the sheets to print
python tools/survey/markers.py detect obs.json photos/               # find every marker in every photograph
python tools/survey/markers.py locate survey.npz obs.json tags.json --distances distances.json
python tools/survey/markers.py control obs_new.json tags.json control.json
python tools/survey/place.py piece/0 survey.npz out.npz --control control.json
```

`detect` needs no help. `locate` fixes each marker on the site grid from every line of
sight to it and checks the survey's size against the tape. `control` and `place.py`
then lay any later set of photographs, reconstructed by itself, onto the same grid by
the markers it shows: the two sets need not share a single picture.

From there the photographs give, in turn: camera positions good to a centimetre or
two; a dense cloud of points on every surface, from which shapes are measured rather
than judged; true-to-scale pictures of each wall and of the ground to trace details
from; and the real surfaces to put on the model.

And a photoreal copy of the place as it stands:

```
python tools/survey/export_colmap.py survey.npz twin --max-side 1600 -- photos/
```

writes the photographs (lens distortion removed) and their cameras as a standard
COLMAP dataset *on the site grid*. A Gaussian-splat trainer run on that folder (Brush
is a free one with a ready-made build for Apple Silicon) learns a scene that can be
walked through and looks like the photographs, and because the cameras were given in
site coordinates it lands on the model with no fitting. That copy cannot be edited the
way the model can. The two are meant to be used together: redesign in the model,
judge it against the copy.
